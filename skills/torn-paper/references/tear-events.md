# Tear events

Pick four events the photograph can actually support. Each event changes which sheet rips. Do not reuse one event with a new color.

A tear belongs to a sheet. Say which sheet, what it separates, and what it must not cross.

## 1. Whole sheet

The hero is one intact sheet in front of everything else. The cream edge follows only the outside silhouette.

Use for a person, a boat, a church, a tower, or a gate that should read as one object.

The tear does not enter a face, a shirt, a window, or a doorway. Flowers, sky, and other buildings stay on the sheets behind the edge.

## 2. Window

The back sheet is torn open. The hero stands whole in front of that opening. A visible paper gap sits between the hero and the nearest background shape.

Use when the hero is in front of foliage, a crowd, or a facade and needs air around the silhouette.

The hole is in the background. It is not a decorative frame around the whole poster, and it is not a rip through the hero.

## 3. Stop

A background mass ends in one torn edge where it meets the hero. The mass comes up to the outline and stops. Nothing from that mass lands on the hero.

Use for a canopy against a face, a hill against a roof, or a cloud bank against a tower.

## 4. Sides

The background is two sheets, left and right. Both tear open toward the middle. The hero stands in the gap, intact.

Use when trees, walls, or buildings flank the subject. Keep a strip of sky or paper between the torn edges and the hero.

## 5. Stack

Three to five horizontal sheets, torn along the photograph's real bands: sky, distance, skyline, water, foreground. The hero sits on one band at the same size as in the photo.

Use for harbors, skylines, and layered landscapes. One tear may reveal a saturated color that is already in the photograph, such as a patch of water. Do not invent a second accent.

## 6. Waterline

One long tear at the horizon. The lower sheet is the reflection: same shapes, darker, sometimes torn shorter than the objects above. The upper sheet holds the place.

Use only when the photograph has water or a wet ground. The reflection stays attached to the same buildings. It does not become a separate abstract stripe.

## 7. Through

A large object lives on a rear sheet. Foreground roofs, beams, or trees are torn sheets that overlap it and leave most of it visible.

Use when something tall rises behind nearer architecture: a wheel, a spire, a tower, a crane. The rear object stays large. The foreground does not shrink into a border.

## 8. Sky lift

The sky is the top sheet. Its lower edge is torn along the roofline, the tree line, or the cloud line. The place is the sheet underneath, full width.

Use when the sky is a distinct color field. The tear follows the skyline. It does not float in empty paper above a tiny building.

## 9. Frame

Trees, clouds, or an arch are separate torn sheets around the hero. They touch the edges of the picture and leave the hero's identifying parts readable: a rose window, a gate, a spire, a face.

Use when the photograph looks out through foliage or an opening. The frame is made of things that are in the photograph. Do not add a wreath.

## People, allowed events

If a person is the hero, use only whole sheet, window, stop, and sides. All four keep the body on one uncut sheet. Do not use stack, waterline, or through if the tear would cross the body.

## Choosing

Before generating, write one line per poster:

```text
1. whole sheet — the church is the front sheet, trees stay behind the torn contour
2. frame — the trees are the torn sheets, the rose window stays readable
3. sky lift — the sky sheet tears along the roof
4. through — the spire is the rear sheet, the nearer roof overlaps it
```

If two lines tear the same sheet in the same place, replace one of them.
